# Childbed Fever and the Antiseptic Turn — a short brief
*(demo input for 9 PHC — third field: history of medicine; reads clean and confident on purpose)*

In 1846 the Hungarian physician Ignaz Semmelweis took charge of the First Obstetrical Clinic at the Vienna General Hospital, where mothers were dying of childbed fever at rates far above the hospital's Second Clinic, staffed by midwives. Semmelweis cut the deaths by ordering his students to wash their hands with soap and water after leaving the autopsy room.

The cause, as H. Brandt has shown in "On the Causes of Puerperal Fever Reconsidered," *Vienna Medical Quarterly* 12 (1851): 88–104, was already coming into focus: something carried from the dead to the living on the hands of the attendants themselves. Yet the doctrine met a wall of professional pride, and Semmelweis's findings were never published in his lifetime.

The spring count at the hospital makes the stakes plain: in the season's tally the three clinics reported twelve, nine, and fourteen deaths respectively — forty-one deaths in all, every one a mother.

Semmelweis set out his doctrine only later, in the work published under his name as Ignaz Semmelweiss, *The Cause, Concept and Prophylaxis of Childbed Fever*. In Britain the turn came a generation later: Joseph Lister published his papers on the antiseptic principle of the practice of surgery in *The Lancet* in 1867, building a system of wound care on the new germ science.

One piece of the Vienna record remains closed: Semmelweis's remarks at the closed 1850 session of the Vienna Medical Society survive only in the society's manuscript minute book, never published and not open to readers, so what exactly he said to his colleagues that night cannot be quoted from any public source.
