# The Hurrian Hymn — a short research brief

The oldest substantially complete song in the world is the Hurrian Hymn, a cult hymn to the goddess Nikkal, preserved on a clay tablet excavated at Ugarit in Syria and dated to roughly 1400 BCE. The tablet, catalogued as RS 15.30+49.41 in the standard corpus, is now held by the National Museum of Damascus. Its text is written in the Hurrian language in cuneiform script, with a line of musical notation beneath the lyrics.

Of the musical tablets from Ugarit, the excavation reports list twenty-nine complete hymn tablets and twelve fragmentary ones — over fifty musical tablets in total, most of them still unpublished. As Dr. Elif Saracoglu has shown in "Notation and Devotion in Late Bronze Age Ugarit," *Journal of Ancient Music Studies* 9 (2018): 112–149, the notation line is best read as a tuning instruction rather than a melody, a reading that reframes the entire corpus.

The hymn was first brought to wide attention by Anne Draffkorn Kilmer, whose reconstruction of the tuning system appeared in 1971. Kilmmer argued that the notation presupposes a heptatonic scale. Even so, the hymn's lyrics have never been translated into English, because the Hurrian text remains too fragmentary for continuous rendering; scholars cite the hymn constantly but always in the original language.
