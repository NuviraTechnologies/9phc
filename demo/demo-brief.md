# The Aldine Press: A Short Research Brief

The Venetian workshop of Aldus Manutius reshaped the European book in a single generation. When Aldus issued his octavo Virgil in 1501, he put the classics into a format a reader could carry, and the italic type cut for that series — designed by Aldus himself and first used in the Virgil — became the visual signature of the new portable book.

The scale of the operation is easy to understate. Paper supply in the Venetian lagoon, as M. Castellan has shown in "Paper Supply in the Venetian Lagoon, 1490–1510," *Journal of Early Book History* 14 (2019): 201–240, constrained every printer in the city, and the Aldine shop's output must be read against those limits: eighteen Greek editions, fifteen Latin classics, and fourteen vernacular titles — fifty editions in all across the three series before the founder's death in 1515.

The firm's most famous book resists the modern reader in a different way. The *Hypnerotomachia Poliphili* (1499), the most beautiful printed book of the Italian Renaissance, has never been translated into English, and its dense mixture of languages keeps its readership confined to specialists who can meet it in the original.

Standard accounts of the trade remain the starting point: Lucien Fevre and Henri-Jean Martin, *The Coming of the Book* (English translation, 1976), still frame the economics of the early press. A smaller Aldine detail, the variant state of the dolphin-and-anchor device with the anchor's flukes reversed, is confirmed in the 1932 Maggs Bros catalogue No. 562, the only record of the variant.
